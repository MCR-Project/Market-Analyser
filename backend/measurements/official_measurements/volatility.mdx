## What it measures

Every other column in this table describes a holding's place inside its
fund — its weight, its correlation, how much of the fund's risk it drives.
This one describes the holding by itself: how much its own price has moved
around, with no reference to the fund at all. A high number means large
swings, up and down; it says nothing about direction.

## Where the numbers come from

`holdings` for the fund's constituents, `price_frame` for each holding's own
closes over the table's shared window (issue #101) — nothing else. No
fund-level data is read at all.

## How it is computed

<Formula tex={String.raw`\sigma = \mathrm{stdev}(\text{scaled returns}) \times \sqrt{252}`} />

`services.stats.volatility` computes this — each return first divided by the
square root of the trading time its own gap covers, so a window spanning
storage tiers (daily, then weekly, then monthly, issue #10) is not
misreported. The same function the portfolio simulator's own Volatility
metric uses on a portfolio's own series; this measurement calls it directly
on one holding's series instead.

## How it reads

There is no universal "good" or "bad" volatility — a low number suits a
reader wanting stability, a high one suits a reader deliberately seeking
larger swings. It is most useful compared across holdings in the same fund,
or against the fund's own volatility (the identity card's own figure).

## Caveats

Null with a reason, not zero, for a holding with fewer than two priced
dates over the window — a two-day-old listing has a return but no
dispersion to measure, and 0% would claim it was riskless rather than
simply short.
