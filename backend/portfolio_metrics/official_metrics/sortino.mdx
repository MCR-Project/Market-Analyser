---
title: Sortino Ratio
summary: The same annualised mean excess return, divided by annualised downside deviation instead of total volatility.
---

## What it measures

Sharpe's sibling, with one change: instead of dividing by total
volatility - which counts an unusually good day exactly the same as an
unusually bad one - Sortino divides by downside deviation, the dispersion
of only the shortfalls below a target. A portfolio that moves up in
occasional large steps and down smoothly looks riskier than it behaved
under Sharpe; Sortino does not penalise it for that.

## How it is computed

<Formula tex={String.raw`\text{Sortino} = \frac{\overline{r} \times 252 - r_f}{\sigma_{\text{downside}}}`} />

The numerator is identical to Sharpe's: the arithmetic mean of the run's
own period returns, annualised, less the risk-free rate for this run's
own window. The denominator is downside deviation against a target of
0% — every period at or above target contributes zero rather than being
dropped from the sample, so a single large loss among many flat days does
not report the same downside risk a series of frequent, similarly sized
ones would.

## How it reads

The same rough scale Sharpe reads on, but Sortino is never lower than
Sharpe for a series whose downside deviation is below its total
volatility and whose excess return is positive - which is most series,
since counting only the shortfalls almost always narrows the
denominator. A Sortino meaningfully higher than the same run's Sharpe
says the volatility Sharpe penalised it for was mostly upside.

## Caveats

Null on the same terms Sharpe is: no risk-free rate available and no
override given, or fewer than two period returns. Also null when nothing
in the window fell short of the target at all - there is no shortfall
left to measure a downside deviation from, not because the run was
riskless, but because everything observed was at or above it.
